# The Control of mRNA Decapping and P-Body Formation

_Molecular Cell, 2008._

DOI: [10.1016/j.molcel.2008.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2008.11.001)

## Summary

This review examines how the cytoplasmic mRNA-decapping machinery is recruited to specific mRNPs and how those mRNPs assemble into processing bodies (PBs). mRNA decapping, the removal of the 5′ m7G cap, is a critical and essentially irreversible step in eukaryotic cytoplasmic mRNA turnover: it simultaneously shuts down cap-dependent translation and exposes the mRNA to 5′-to-3′ exonucleolytic degradation by Xrn1. Decapping is catalyzed by Dcp2 together with its coactivator Dcp1 and is stimulated by a set of decapping enhancers (Edc1/2/3, Hedls/Ge-1/Edc4, the Lsm1-7–Pat1 complex, and the DExD/H-box helicase Dhh1/RCK/p54). Because the cap is also bound and protected by the eIF4E/eIF4F translation-initiation complex, decapping and translation are framed as competing pathways: the cap-binding complex must first be displaced before Dcp2 can act. mRNPs that have recruited the decapping machinery and are translationally repressed can self-assemble into PBs. Franks and Lykke-Andersen propose a kinetic model in which the extent of PB formation is directly proportional to the cytoplasmic concentration of repressed mRNPs and is governed by the competing relative rates of translational repression, PB-factor recruitment / mRNP multimerization, and mRNA decay. They argue PBs are dynamic self-assembling clusters rather than preformed organelles, and they review the unresolved controversy over whether PBs are sites of mRNA decay, of storage and recycling back to polysomes, or merely a consequence of repressed-mRNP accumulation.

## Key Points

- **Decapping engine = Dcp2 + Dcp1.** Dcp2 is the catalytic subunit, carrying an N-terminal Nudix/MutT motif whose three conserved glutamates coordinate a divalent cation for cap hydrolysis; it releases m7GDP and a 5′-monophosphorylated mRNA body, an irreversible step that commits the mRNA to Xrn1 degradation. Dcp1 is the coactivator; in yeast it strongly stimulates Dcp2 catalysis (driving an open→closed conformational change) with little effect on cap binding.
- **Dcp2 contacts the mRNA twice.** Efficient decapping requires contact through the m7G cap and through the 5′ end of the mRNA body (a conserved channel in the Nudix domain); short RNAs or RNAs with a 5′-hybridized DNA oligo are poorly decapped, predicting that anything bound at the cap/5′ end (e.g., eIF4F) protects the mRNA.
- **Metazoan twist — Hedls/Ge-1/Edc4.** Human Dcp1 does not stably bind or stimulate Dcp2; instead the metazoan-specific protein Hedls (Ge-1/Edc4) stimulates Dcp2 activity and bridges the Dcp1–Dcp2 association. Whether Hedls is a core "Dcp" or a peripheral "Edc" enhancer was left open.
- **Translation vs decapping competition.** The decapping complex competes with the eIF4F cap-binding complex (stabilized by eIF4G–PABP–poly(A) closed-loop interactions) for the cap. Decapping therefore proceeds in two steps: (1) displace the cap-binding translation factors (translational repression), then (2) Dcp2 binds the cap and catalyzes hydrolysis.
- **Cap-step enhancers (Edc1, Edc2, Edc3).** Yeast-specific Edc1/Edc2 stimulate Dcp2 on naked RNA in vitro (acting at the cap-binding/catalysis step). Edc3 is conserved across eukaryotes, interacts directly with Dcp1 and Dcp2, and is thought to recruit/activate the decapping complex on a subset of mRNAs.
- **Repression-coupled enhancers (Dhh1 and Pat1).** Dhh1 (orthologs RCK/p54, Me31B, CGH-1, Xp54) is a DExD/H-box ATPase and general translational repressor; Pat1, with the Lsm1-7 complex, binds deadenylated mRNAs and promotes their decapping. Both couple translational repression to decapping; codepletion blocks the global translational repression seen on glucose starvation.
- **Deadenylation as a decapping precursor.** Deadenylation is a major, often rate-limiting, trigger for yeast decapping and can trigger it in mammals, but does not always lead to decapping — stably repressed deadenylated mRNAs exist (oocyte maturation, neurons), possibly protected by locked-down cap complexes or by cap-binding deadenylases such as PARN.
- **Transcript-specific decapping recruitment.** Specialized decay pathways recruit the decapping machinery: NMD via Upf1, ARE-mediated decay via TTP/BRF-1/KSRP (TTP gives ARE-dependent Dcp2 activation in vitro), miRNA/RISC silencing (Dhh1-dependent), PUF proteins, autoregulated yeast transcripts (Rps28b, Yra1, EDC1 via Edc3), sequence-specific Dcp2 targets, and oligo/poly-U tails. General decapping inhibitors also exist (e.g., testis-specific VCX-A competing for the cap).
- **PBs are self-assembling clusters of repressed mRNPs, not preformed organelles.** PB formation correlates directly with the cytoplasmic pool of ribosome-free repressed mRNPs: cycloheximide (trapping mRNAs in polysomes) dissolves PBs; releasing mRNAs from polysomes or slowing post-release decay steps (e.g., inactivating Dcp2 or Xrn1) increases PBs; PB integrity is RNA-dependent (RNase disassembles them).
- **Assembly is driven by multimerization domains.** Yeast Edc3 (Yjef-N dimerization domain, in the decapping complex) and Lsm4 (Q/N prion-like domain, in the Lsm-Pat1 complex) mediate the mRNP–mRNP interactions needed for PB assembly; deleting both abolishes visible PBs without affecting translation or decay. In metazoans, Hedls and GW182 carry candidate Q/N-rich assembly domains; Lsm4's prion-like domain is largely replaced by an RGG domain.
- **Two fates from the PB: decay or recycling.** mRNPs in PBs can either be degraded or released back into the translational pool (consistent with rapid FRAP cycling of PB factors, sub-minute half-lives; yeast mRNAs re-engage translation when glucose returns; a miRNA-targeted human mRNA re-enters translation upon HuR binding). Direct evidence that mRNAs are actually degraded while inside a PB had not yet been shown.
- **Function of PB assembly remains controversial.** Loss of microscopically visible PBs has not been shown to inhibit any specific decay or repression pathway, so PB assembly may not be required for repression/degradation of tested mRNAs — though loss of visible PBs does not equal loss of functional PBs. PBs might instead sequester decay enzymes away from translating mRNAs, or concentrate Dcp2 locally for limiting decay pathways.

## Key Model

**The kinetic model for P-body formation (Figure 2).** PB assembly is not localization of mRNPs onto a pre-existing organelle but the self-assembly of translationally repressed mRNPs, and its extent is directly proportional to the cytoplasmic concentration of those repressed mRNPs. The outcome is set by competing relative rates of:

1. **Translational repression** — an mRNA must first be released from polysomes / silenced (e.g., by Dhh1 and Pat1, likely the first PB factors recruited). Repression is necessary but, in human/Drosophila reporters, not always sufficient: cis-elements that recruit PB factors may also be required, suggesting repression and PB-factor recruitment can be separable steps.
2. **PB-factor recruitment / mRNP multimerization** — repressed mRNPs form a PB "monomer" and then multimerize via assembly domains (Edc3 Yjef-N, Lsm4 Q/N, and candidate Hedls/GW182 domains), possibly through mutually exclusive homomeric interactions that also keep PBs distinct from stress granules.
3. **mRNA decay** — if decay enzymes are not limiting and decay is rapid, mRNAs are degraded before they can assemble; if decay enzymes are limiting, repressed mRNPs accumulate and assemble into PBs, where they are then either degraded or released back to polysomes.

Two consequences the authors stress: (a) because PBs are a heterogeneous, dynamically cycling pool, any perturbation that shifts the global repressed-mRNP concentration changes PB visibility for *all* mRNPs — so disappearance of visible PBs (e.g., on miRNA-pathway or deadenylation blockade) does not by itself prove those processes are required for PB assembly; and (b) light microscopy and extract-based fractionation cannot reliably quantify the PB-localized fraction, since extract preparation perturbs the rapid mRNP equilibrium.

## Open Questions

- Are all cellular translationally repressed mRNPs competent for PB assembly, or only a specific subset?
- Do all repressed mRNPs assemble via the same protein domain, or do multiple assembly domains (Edc3, Lsm4, Hedls, GW182) direct PB assembly in parallel?
- How are PB-associated mRNPs "marked" for either degradation or storage/recycling back into translation? Does the Dcp2 open→closed structural rearrangement act as the decapping commitment switch?
- Can mRNAs actually be degraded while assembled inside a PB? Is PB-factor cycling (photoactivation/FRAP) dependent on decay-enzyme catalytic activity?
- What, if any, is the conserved function of mRNP assembly into PBs in promoting repression, decapping, or decay — and why is the property evolutionarily conserved if visible PBs are dispensable for tested pathways?
- How is translational repression coordinated with decapping activation across the various transcript-specific decay pathways (NMD, ARE, miRNA, PUF)?
- What determines whether deadenylated mRNAs are handed to the decapping machinery versus kept stably repressed (deadenylase identity, e.g., PARN; cap lock-down)?
- How do distinct mRNP granules (PBs, stress granules, germ-cell and neuronal granules) coexist in one cytoplasm, and how are specific assembly complexes targeted to individual mRNPs?
