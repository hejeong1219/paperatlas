# P-Bodies: Composition, Properties, and Functions

_Biochemistry, 2018._

## Summary

This Perspective (Biochemistry 2018, 57, 2424−2431, part of the "Membrane-Less Organelles" special issue) reviews recent advances in understanding processing bodies (P-bodies): cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein (RNP) granules composed primarily of translationally repressed mRNAs in complex with proteins of the translational-repression and 5′-to-3′ mRNA-decay machinery. P-bodies are conserved across eukaryotes and share with other membraneless RNP granules (Cajal bodies, nucleoli, stress granules) a dependence on protein–RNA interaction networks, low-complexity sequences, and liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) for assembly, yet each has a distinct composition and likely function. The authors update P-body composition from recent targeted and high-throughput proteomics/transcriptomics, summarize direct evidence that P-bodies and their constituents undergo LLPS, review the interactions and post-translational modifications that regulate P-body assembly/disassembly, and re-examine the long-standing "storage versus decay" debate over P-body function. They conclude that, while neither model can be fully excluded, accumulating evidence (live-cell single-molecule mRNA-decay imaging showing decay throughout the cytoplasm rather than in granules; intact, non-5′-truncated mRNA in purified P-bodies; enzyme inhibition inside reconstituted droplets) is most consistent with P-bodies acting as storage granules for translationally repressed mRNAs and inactive decay enzymes, with catalysis of mRNA decay occurring throughout the cytoplasm.

## Key Points

- **Definition and conservation**: P-bodies are cytoplasmic RNP granules comprised primarily of translationally repressed mRNAs plus proteins associated with translational repression and 5′-to-3′ mRNA decay; they are conserved in eukaryotes and resemble other RNP granules (Cajal bodies, nucleoli, stress granules) that depend on protein–RNA networks, low-complexity sequences, and LLPS.
- **Distinct from related granules**: stress granules share some components and can contact P-bodies and be co-induced by stress, but uniquely contain translation-initiation factors; AGO2/GW182 localize to P-bodies only in metazoans, and "GW-bodies" were later shown to colocalize with multivesicular bodies (not P-bodies) in higher eukaryotes.
- **Core composition**: constitutive P-body proteins are largely deadenylation/decay factors — the Ccr4-Not deadenylation complex, Lsm1-7, the decapping enzyme/coactivator Dcp1/Dcp2, decapping activators Edc3, Pat1, DDX6 (Rck/p54; Dhh1p in yeast) and EDC4, and the 5′-to-3′ exoribonuclease Xrn1 — plus translational repressors such as 4E-T and CPEB1.
- **Newly identified components**: mass-spec proteomics and targeted studies added factors including NoBody/NBDY (a 69-aa human microprotein from LINC01420 that copurifies with the decapping complex and photo-cross-links to EDC4), the m6A "reader" YTHDF2, and CCHCR1 (interacts with EDC4); a FAPS-based P-body purification (Hubstenberger et al.) revealed RNA-modifying enzymes (TUT4, APOBEC3F), RNA-binding proteins (PUM1/2, STAU2, HNRNPU), and, surprisingly, myosins (e.g., MYO6), hinting at a cytoskeletal link.
- **P-bodies sequester ~1/5 of cellular RNAs**: concurrent sequencing of sorted P-body RNAs identified roughly one-fifth of cellular RNAs, many of them intact, translationally repressed mRNAs linked to regulatory processes.
- **Direct evidence of liquid-droplet/LLPS behavior**: live-cell microscopy shows P-bodies are spherical, can relax and fuse into larger round bodies, undergo rapid FRAP exchange of proteins/RNA with the cytoplasm, and can be reversibly dissolved by 1,6-hexanediol — all hallmarks of liquids.
- **In vitro reconstitution of LLPS**: purified P-body proteins phase separate (e.g., the Lsm4 IDR forms droplets that mature into amyloid-like fibrils; Edc3 + a Dcp2 fragment, GST-Pdc1 + Edc3, and a Dcp1/Dcp2/Edc3 ternary system phase separate), driven by multivalent helical leucine-rich motifs (HLMs) and enhanced by Edc3 IDRs, added RNA, or low salt; phase diagrams suggest cellular concentrations sit near the LLPS boundary.
- **Assembly is network-driven and partly redundant**: P-body maintenance depends on low-complexity/RNA-binding domains (Edc3 Yjef-N, Lsm4 Q/N-rich and human Lsm4 RGG, DDX6) and on translationally repressed mRNA (cycloheximide trapping mRNA in polysomes dissolves P-bodies); a yeast edc3Δ lsm4Δ strain can still form P-bodies via alternative multivalent interactions (Dhh1 overexpression, Psp2, Pby1), indicating the interaction network matters more than any single component.
- **Energy-dependent remodeling and PTMs regulate assembly**: ATPase activity (e.g., Dhh1) maintains P-body integrity/fluidity (hyperactivity dissolves them); phosphorylation (Dcp2 by Ste20; Dcp1A), K63-polyubiquitylation of Dcp1A (dominant-negative ubiquitin abolishes P-bodies), and PRMT5-mediated arginine dimethylation of the Lsm4 RGG domain modulate P-body formation.
- **Storage-vs-decay debate**: macroscopically observable P-bodies are NOT required for mRNA decay, mRNAs can recycle from P-bodies back to translating polysomes, and decay still occurs in yeast lacking functional edc3 and lsm4 (no visible P-bodies), supporting a storage model alongside the older "site of decay" hypothesis.
- **New mRNA-decay evidence favors storage**: single-molecule 5′-to-3′ decay reporters in human cells and a re-engineered MS2 system in yeast show decay products appear throughout the cytoplasm, not in granules; purified P-bodies contain intact, non-5′-truncated mRNA (only greater polyA-length variance); and Dcp2 (and even RNase A) activity is suppressed inside reconstituted droplets — together inconsistent with decay occurring within P-bodies.
- **Functional uncoupling**: deleting the Lsm4 RGG domain abolishes visible P-bodies without affecting mRNA decay or translational repression, decoupling P-body structure from these functions.

## Scope

Covers: molecular composition of P-bodies (constitutive decay/repression machinery plus newly identified proteins from targeted and high-throughput proteomics/transcriptomics, including microproteins, m6A readers, RNA-modifying enzymes, and myosins); direct in-cell and in-vitro evidence that P-bodies and their constituents undergo LLPS; the protein–protein and protein–RNA interaction network, redundancy, ATPase-driven remodeling, and PTMs that regulate P-body assembly/disassembly; and a critical re-examination of P-body function (storage vs. site of mRNA decay) in light of single-molecule decay imaging, purified-P-body RNA analysis, and droplet-reconstitution enzymology. As a Perspective it summarizes advances since the prior major field overview rather than providing a comprehensive component catalog (it defers to earlier reviews for the full protein list) and does not present new primary experimental data.

## Open Questions

- If P-bodies are mRNA-storage granules, the abundant, RNA-associated decapping/decay enzymes within them must be inhibited so most transcripts are protected — by what mechanism is this inhibition achieved?
- By extension, by what mechanism is mRNA-decay enzymatic activity enhanced in the cytoplasm outside P-bodies?
- What is the molecular mechanism of the apparent general suppression of enzymatic activity (e.g., Dcp2, RNase A) inside liquid droplets?
- Given the lability and rapid cytoplasmic exchange of P-body factors, which additional P-body-resident proteins remain to be purified and identified, and how would the full composition refine views of P-body biochemical activity?
- Could P-bodies fulfill some cellular role unrelated to mRNA decay, in which case how is resident mRNA protected from enzymatic degradation?
- How exactly do PTMs (phosphorylation, ubiquitylation, arginine methylation) and energy-dependent remodeling reshape the protein–protein/protein–RNA network to control P-body assembly and LLPS?
